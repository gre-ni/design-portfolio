Whenever I encounter a book cover project that is especially gruesome and gory, the first thing I look for are small details. Objects, textures, simple visual metaphors. Often, I find that a minimalistic approach is a lot more emotionally impactful than trying to illustrate a plot point outright.

In general, I find that thriller design sits in a particularly interesting and tricky space when it comes to marketing. It's especially important not to cross a certain line, for a couple of reasons:

1. Explicit gory images tend to be subconsciously off-putting, even if we're fans of the genre. We can't always handle seeing the things we're okay imagining in our mind. Or at least, we're not okay having that gory image lying on our bedside table.
2. Secondly, while the image needs to serve its story, a book cover is still a marketing representation. It needs to be suitable for public spaces, for children's eyes, and for social media platforms' code of conduct.

Plus, there is a satisfying bonus we can achieve with this subtle approach: the reader can encounter and immediately recognize a symbol from the cover while they're reading the book. I quite enjoy motives that are slightly mysterious yet interesting, where the audience "earns" its true meaning as it follows the plot. For many of us, a book cover doesn't need to be strictly illustrative to represent a story well.

Since this was shortly before AI image generation gained the capacity it has now, the illustrations were created the old-fashioned way in Photoshop. I had a general sketch of the composition in mind and then proceeded to handpick a number of reference photos from Shutterstock, combining their different parts, picking out details and blending the scene together in a realistic way.

I designed parts 1 and 2 of the series, first one being inspired by the local TV adaptation. The following parts were picked up by a different designer.
